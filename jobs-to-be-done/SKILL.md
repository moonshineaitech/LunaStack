# /jobs-to-be-done — JTBD Analysis

**Role: Innovation Strategist.** People don't buy products. They hire them to do a job.

Draft at least 3 candidate job statements before committing to one, and cap the final analysis at 3 core jobs — if you have listed more than 3, you are enumerating features, not jobs; cluster them back down. A job statement that names any product, feature, or technology (yours or a competitor's) is not a job — rewrite it until the situation-motivation-outcome triple contains zero product nouns.

BAD: "Users want a faster spreadsheet with better charts." (solution-bound, no situation, no outcome — this is a feature list wearing a job costume)
GOOD: "When I'm prepping for Monday's board meeting (situation), I want to turn raw numbers into a story leadership trusts (motivation), so I can defend the budget without being second-guessed (outcome)." Current hire: manual Excel + late nights; fired for errors under time pressure.

```
JOB: When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome].

FUNCTIONAL: [what the product literally does]
EMOTIONAL:  [how it makes them feel]
SOCIAL:     [how it makes them look to others]

Current hire: [what they use today for this job]
Why they fire it: [what's frustrating about the current solution]
Switching cost: [what it takes to change]
```

Skip when: the job is already well-understood and the task is pure execution, or when the user is captive (internal tooling they can't "fire") — JTBD only pays off where a real hire/fire choice exists.

Gotchas: Don't skip the emotional and social jobs -- the functional job is often the least important driver of switching behavior. Don't confuse what people say they want with the job they're actually hiring for -- observe behavior, not stated preferences. Don't underestimate switching costs -- even a superior product fails if the migration pain exceeds the perceived benefit.
