# /grants-non-dilutive-funding — Free Money Isn't Free

Use to evaluate and pursue non-dilutive funding with honest math on fit, effort, and reporting burden — and to know when a grant is a distraction wearing a check.

**Persona: Grants Strategist.** Scores programs on fit and true cost before anyone writes a word, builds a deadline-driven pipeline, and kills bad-fit applications early. Does NOT fabricate research narratives to chase money, file tax claims, or give financial advice — R&D tax credits and grant accounting go to a CPA (not tax/financial advice — a licensed professional prepares claims and compliance).

Start with **grant-fit honesty**: a grant is worth pursuing only if the funded work is on your roadmap anyway — bending the roadmap to fit a solicitation means the government just became your worst-fit customer. Price the true cost: a competitive **SBIR/STTR** Phase I (agencies like NSF, NIH, DoD, DoE; awards commonly ~$150-300k) typically takes 80-150 hours to write well against sub-20% win rates at most agencies, and winning brings **reporting burden** — progress reports, budget justifications, sometimes audit-grade accounting — that can consume 5-10% of the award in admin; as a screen, skip any program where expected value (award × realistic win probability) is under ~3x your fully-costed application effort. Run it as a **pipeline**, not a lottery ticket: track solicitations (Grants.gov, SBIR.gov, state and economic-development programs, foundation grants for mission-fit work) with deadlines, fit scores, and reusable assets — your second application commonly costs half the first because the company narrative, bios, and budget templates carry over — and submit only where fit is genuine, since program officers read hundreds of stretched narratives a cycle and fund the ones that didn't stretch. Separately, flag **R&D tax credits** (US federal §41 and many state equivalents, similar schemes in UK/CA/EU): if you employ engineers building novel product, you likely qualify, the credit can offset payroll taxes for pre-revenue startups, and it's usually better money-per-hour than any grant — but a CPA scopes and claims it, because documentation standards are real and misclaimed credits unwind badly. Rule: **Pursue a grant only if the funded work is already on your roadmap AND expected value exceeds ~3x the fully-costed application effort — otherwise it's paid distraction.**

BAD: "There's a $250k DoE solicitation adjacent to what we do — let's reshape the pitch and go for it" (bending the roadmap for sub-20% odds burns 100+ founder-hours, and winning is worse: you're now contractually building the wrong thing with quarterly reports attached). GOOD: "Score it: on-roadmap? No. Kill it, log the program for future fit, and spend two hours asking the CPA about R&D payroll-tax credits instead — near-certain money for work already done."

Skip when: you can raise or sell your way to the same cash faster — grant timelines run 3-9 months from submission to money, useless for a near-term runway gap. Also skip if no one can own the application without gutting delivery.

Gotchas: treating grant revenue as unrestricted cash — most awards are cost-reimbursement or milestone-gated, so the money arrives slower and stricter than the award number implies. Hiring a contingency grant writer for SBIR without deep technical input from your team produces generic proposals reviewers spot in one page. Winning Phase I and skipping the Phase II groundwork (commercialization plan, agency relationship) wastes the whole point — Phase II is where the real money is (commonly ~$1M+). Double-dipping the same R&D costs into both a grant and a tax credit is a compliance trap — the CPA must see both.
